From July 3–17, 2025, I was part of a cetacean ecology survey aboard the NOAA Ship *Henry B. Bigelow*, working the shelf break south of Cape Cod in search of one of the hardest groups of whales to study in the North Atlantic: beaked whales.

The cruise focused on offshore waters of Georges Bank, including slope waters near the shelf edge, where our team combined visual surveys, passive acoustics, prey sampling, and drone operations to better understand deep-diving cetaceans in this region. We sailed out to the edge of the continental shelf roughly 100 miles from shore and tracked along the shelf break looking for species that are far more often heard than seen. The survey area included the waters of Georges Bank within the U.S. EEZ, from approximately 39°–41° N and 64°–70.5° W.

![](/images/beaked_whales/bigelow.jpeg)

The *Henry B. Bigelow* was a fitting platform for this kind of work. [NOAA’s ship page](https://www.omao.noaa.gov/marine-operations/ships/henry-b-bigelow) describes the vessel as 208.6 feet long and was built as a fisheries survey ship with a low-acoustic-signature design for research. It supports fisheries, oceanographic, and marine mammal work along the U.S. East Coast.

Our targets included goose-beaked whales (*Ziphius cavirostris*), Sowerby’s beaked whales (*Mesoplodon bidens*), and True’s beaked whales (*Mesoplodon mirus*). The cruise report notes that all three species were detected acoustically during the survey, and that goose-beaked whales were also the most frequently sighted beaked whale visually. These are elusive offshore animals associated with deep water, long dives, and brief, cryptic surfacings. NOAA species pages for [Cuvier’s / goose-beaked whale](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/cuviers-beaked-whale), [Sowerby’s beaked whale](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/sowerbys-beaked-whale), and [True’s beaked whale](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/trues-beaked-whale) give a sense of just how little is still known about them.

That challenge defined the cruise. During daylight hours, observers worked from the flying bridge using 25x150 “big-eye” binoculars and handheld binoculars, scanning for surfacings, blows, or any small cue that a whale might be present. At the same time, a towed passive acoustic array listened underwater while we surveyed. Someone had to monitor continuously for the characteristic calls of the beaked whale species we were interested in among the noise of the ocean and the sounds of other marine life, including sperm whales and dolphins.

![](/images/beaked_whales/C5D_1158.JPG)

We had a few visual sightings and would divert toward the animals, trying to resight them and stay with them long enough to do something useful. That was easier said than done. Beaked whales surface quietly, show little, and then disappear into long dives. We wanted to relocate them, get close enough, and get the drone airborne and over them for photogrammetry. But again and again, by the time we thought we had a chance, they were gone.

![](/images/beaked_whales/IMG_0078.JPG)

For the photogrammetry effort, we were flying a [FreeFly Astro](https://freeflysystems.com/astro) equipped with a Sony A7R IV and Sigma 24 mm mapping lens, along with a LightWare laser altimeter to record height off the water. The short surface intervals of goose-beaked whales—typically just 2–3 minutes—meant that success depended on a predictive launch strategy. We had to estimate when and where the whales would surface and already be in position when they came up.

After days of minimal visual opportunities, everything finally came together on **July 14, 2025**.

We got onto a group of goose-beaked whales and, after watching them long enough, started to get a feel for their rhythm. Once they dove, we were able to predict when they were likely to resurface and where. That was the opening we had been waiting for.

On the first flight, I launched the Astro and pushed out toward the group, but I was flying directly into the sun. Target acquisition was extremely difficult. By the time I could sort out where the whales were in the glare, the opportunity was gone and the group submerged again. Because of how difficult the earlier encounters had been, there was a real concern that this had been our only chance.

But then the whales surfaced again only about 15 minutes later.

This time, we used that short interval to our advantage. I had the drone in the air *before* they resurfaced. When the call came from the visual team, I was already positioned to fly out to them. I got the Astro over the group and managed to capture images of all six whales together at the surface. In total, the second sortie obtained 102 high-resolution nadir images over roughly three minutes from 45 m altitude, documenting all six individuals.

![](/images/beaked_whales/DSC00033.JPG)
*Photograph taken under MMPA Permit #27066*

What stood out immediately was the coloration of the animals. The males had extensive scarring, so much of their bodies appeared white. In the imagery, the whales looked almost carved. The resolution of the camera was high enough that I could make out the depth and texture of individual scars, along with the signature pair of forward-projecting teeth of the goose-beaked whale.

It was an incredible opportunity to fly over these animals, especially after such difficult survey conditions. Goose-beaked whales are among the most elusive cetaceans in the world, and getting a clean aerial view of six individuals together at the surface felt like one of those rare field moments where persistence finally meets opportunity.
