My master's project at Duke University examined how Bird Shoal and the broader Rachel Carson Reserve changed between 2017/2018 and 2023. I focused on the Rachel Carson Reserve, an inlet barrier island complex in North Carolina's Inner Banks on the Crystal Coast, just south of Beaufort and directly landward of Beaufort Inlet.

This project sits at the intersection of coastal geomorphology, remote sensing, and applied management. In plain language, **geomorphology** is the study of landforms and how they change through processes like waves, tides, wind, and sediment movement. I wanted to understand how a protected-looking barrier island system was actually behaving over time, and what that could mean for habitat change and future management.

*This master's project was submitted in partial fulfillment of the Master of Environmental Management degree at Duke University in April 2024. It was not peer reviewed or formally published. [Read the full project](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/919cb1a3-c0a4-4c56-9a6c-66d01718462e/content).*

## Why this place matters

Barrier islands are sandy landforms that help absorb storm energy and protect the areas behind them. The Rachel Carson Reserve (RCR) includes three main inlet barrier islands: Town Marsh, Carrot Island, and Bird Shoal. Of those three, Bird Shoal is the most exposed to wave action moving through Beaufort Inlet, so it acts as the Reserve's first line of defense during storms.

Earlier research had suggested that Bird Shoal was no longer behaving like a typical **fetch-limited barrier island**, or FLBI. A fetch-limited barrier island usually sits in a relatively sheltered setting where day-to-day wave energy is low and major change is expected mostly during storms. Bird Shoal, however, appeared to be changing even outside of those expectations. My project tested that idea with a new survey and a broader look at how both landform and habitat were shifting across the Reserve.

## What I did

I combined drone mapping, elevation analysis, shoreline analysis, and habitat classification to compare conditions from 2017/2018 to 2023.

- I used drone imagery and [structure-from-motion photogrammetry](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/structure-motion-sfm-photogrammetric-processing-historical-aerial-photographs) to build high-resolution orthomosaics and digital surface models of Bird Shoal and the surrounding Reserve.
- I used the [Digital Shoreline Analysis System (DSAS)](https://www.usgs.gov/index.php/centers/whcmsc/science/digital-shoreline-analysis-system-dsas) to measure how far Bird Shoal's shoreline moved and how quickly those changes occurred.
- I compared elevation models from 2018 and 2023 to estimate where Bird Shoal lost sediment, where it gained sediment, and how its dune system changed.
- I classified habitat across the Reserve using Sentinel-2 imagery informed by drone imagery, building on [Gray et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs10081257), which provided the earlier habitat baseline for the Rachel Carson Reserve.

![Comparison of drone imagery and Sentinel-2 imagery used for habitat classification.](/images/projects/RCR/Figure2.jpg)

## What I found

The short version is that Bird Shoal was not simply eroding away. It was moving, reshaping, and in some places growing at the same time.

- Most shoreline transects showed erosion, with about 77% of measured transects migrating landward between 2018 and 2023.
- Even so, Bird Shoal's area above the 0.50 m contour increased from 39.1 to 48.0 hectares, a gain of 8.9 hectares.
- The island elongated eastward by about 293 meters over the study period.
- Bird Shoal lost about 131,662 cubic meters of sediment in some places but gained about 242,743 cubic meters in others, for a net gain of about 111,080 cubic meters.
- Dunes on parts of West and Central Bird Shoal widened and increased in elevation, while other sections, especially farther east, experienced strong landward retreat and overwash.
- Across the wider Reserve, emergent wetland gained 5.47 hectares from 2017 to 2023, suggesting that geomorphic change on Bird Shoal was already influencing habitat change elsewhere in the system.

![Shoreline change transects across Bird Shoal from 2018 to 2023.](/images/projects/RCR/Figure4.jpg)

That combination of retreat, elongation, dune growth, overwash, and wetland expansion is why I describe Bird Shoal as behaving more like a **mixed-energy** barrier island than a classic sheltered FLBI. In other words, it appears to be shaped by both local sheltered-water processes and stronger ocean wave energy entering through Beaufort Inlet.

## Why I think that happened

My interpretation is that widening at Beaufort Inlet changed the way wave energy reached Bird Shoal. Earlier work had already linked inlet change to unusual geomorphic behavior on the island, and my results support that idea. As the inlet widened and nearby ocean-facing barriers changed, Bird Shoal seems to have been exposed to a different wave pattern than the one expected for a sheltered estuarine island.

That helps explain why I found a landscape that was doing several things at once: retreating landward in many places, building dunes in others, extending eastward, and sending overwash sediment into lower areas behind the island. Those back-barrier deposits appear to be helping create conditions where marsh habitat can expand.

## Why it matters for management

For me, this was not just an academic exercise. The Rachel Carson Reserve protects habitat, supports research, and helps buffer Beaufort from coastal hazards. If Bird Shoal keeps migrating landward while also growing dunes and redistributing sediment, managers need to know which changes increase resilience and which ones increase vulnerability.

One of the clearest takeaways is that Bird Shoal's dunes may be growing stronger in some sections, which could improve its ability to trap sand and act as a barrier. At the same time, low or broken dune sections still leave the island vulnerable to overwash and possible breaching during major storms. My project suggests that continued monitoring, and potentially targeted dune-stabilizing vegetation in vulnerable gaps, could help managers track and reduce those risks.

The habitat results matter too. Marsh expansion can increase ecological value and help stabilize the back-barrier environment, but shifts in woody vegetation, sand cover, and wetland boundaries can also change the kinds of species the Reserve can support. In that sense, geomorphic change and habitat change are tightly linked.

## Limits of the study

This was a master's project, not a peer-reviewed publication, and it was designed as a synoptic comparison rather than an event-by-event storm analysis. That means I could identify important patterns over roughly five years, but I could not always tie each landform change to one exact storm or forcing condition. I also note in the full write-up that the volumetric method likely overestimates some gains and losses because it focuses on land above a 0.50 m threshold.

Even with those caveats, the project gave me a strong applied framework for combining fieldwork, drone mapping, geospatial analysis, and coastal interpretation in a management-relevant setting.
